How to Use Claude for More Than Your Resume: A Portfolio, Built as a Tutorial

August 17, 2026

In 2023, I got laid off for the first time. Community manager at Red Hat, gone. I found new work fast, landed my highest-paying role yet at Sauce Labs—then got laid off from that too, nine months in, along with my whole team. Six months of unemployment followed. The lesson wasn’t that I was a failure or that my employers sucked. It was that when you stop pouring into yourself, you lose sight of true purpose—like a hamster on a hamster wheel. I realized my biggest deficit wasn’t cash flow or competence. It was that I didn’t have my own goals. I don’t think I even knew who I was outside of a job.

So when I decided to prove I could do more than survive another layoff, I didn’t start with a resume. I started with Claude, and I built this site—the one you might be looking at right now—to show what I actually know how to do. This post is that process, step by step, written for anyone who thinks Claude is just a tool for polishing cover letters.

If that’s you—you’ve used Claude to fix a typo or reword a bullet point, and stopped there—here’s what you’re missing.

1. Start with a real goal, not a prompt

I didn’t open Claude and type “build me a website.” I opened it with an actual goal: turn a rough, painful stretch of my career into something a hiring manager could click through and believe. The goal came first. The tool came second. If you start with “what should I ask Claude to do,” you’ll get something generic. Start with what you actually need to be true on the other side, and work backward.

2. Let it ask you questions first

Before Claude wrote a single word of my About Me page, it asked me what “shorter” meant to me—one paragraph, or three? For hiring managers, or for readers who want the real story, or both? I could have skipped straight to a draft. I didn’t, because the first draft is only as good as the constraints you gave it.

3. Draft together, then say what’s wrong

My first trimmed About Me page was shorter, but it didn’t sound like me anymore—all my jokes gone, smoothed into something a corporate blog would post. I said so, directly: “My tone is gone. Trim the fat, but maintain my prose as much as possible.” That one sentence turned a forgettable rewrite into something I’d actually publish.

4. Keep your own voice, not Claude’s polish

Left alone, Claude defaults toward clean and safe. That’s not always what you want. The About Me page that’s actually live still has the line “shit’s creek without a paddle” in it, because I said keep it. If a draft comes back sounding like nobody, tell it to sound like you specifically.

5. Know when to switch tools

Partway through, I asked why Claude couldn’t see the code I was also editing in Claude Code, a separate tool that works directly on my computer. The honest answer: this session runs in an isolated sandbox on Anthropic’s servers, walled off from my Mac by design. Claude Code runs locally and touches my real files directly. Once I understood that split, I stopped expecting one tool to do both.

6. Pick one source of truth

Once I connected my Mac folder, I found my homepage headline had quietly drifted between two copies of the same file. Nothing was broken, but I was maintaining two versions without meaning to. I picked one—the real files Claude Code touches—and deleted the duplicate.

7. Turn your own process into material

Partway through today, I realized I was about to forget every decision that got me here. So instead of writing this post from memory afterward, I had Claude build me a small tool: I say “log this,” and it writes a dated entry the moment the decision happens. This post exists because of that file.

This tutorial wasn’t written after the fact. It was built the same way it describes—today, in real time, one decision at a time. If you want to see the result, watch my site unfold: this entire build is the proof that this process works.
